Lore Sources

The real history and the old myths under every part of LETHE, and why each one is there.

How to read this

Fact stays fact. Fiction is labelled.

LETHE works because players can look things up and find they're real. That only works if we never blur the line. In the game, anything goes. In our public content (the "Erased" videos, the store page, interviews) we state history accurately and say clearly which parts are our story.

Each source below has the same fields: what happened, what historians still argue about, how LETHE uses it, and why it earns its place.

Part one

The myths of forgetting

These give LETHE its rules. Many cultures imagined a river or a drink that wipes memory before the next life. That's why the idea feels familiar to everyone.

MythGreece · c. 700 BC onward

Lethe, the river of forgetting

The story
Lethe is one of the five rivers of the Greek underworld, with the Styx, Acheron, Cocytus and Phlegethon. Hesiod names Lethe as a child of Eris (Strife). In Plato's Republic (Book X), the soldier Er sees the dead cross the Plain of Lethe and drink from the River of Unmindfulness before rebirth. In Virgil's Aeneid (Book VI), souls crowd the banks of the Lethe to drink and forget before returning to life.
In LETHE
The world's name and its first law: what is forgotten goes to Lethe.
Why it's in
People already half-know the idea, and it has 2,500 years of literature behind it. Players feel the depth without needing it explained.
MythNewGreece and southern Italy · c. 400 BC onward

Mnemosyne and the gold tablets

The story
Mnemosyne is the Titan goddess of memory and mother of the nine Muses. Thin gold tablets buried with followers of the Orphic mysteries tell the dead what to do in the underworld: avoid the first spring, beside a white cypress, and ask instead to drink from the lake of Memory. Drinking from Memory meant keeping who you were.
In LETHE
Proposal: the Firstlight is what happens to someone who, once, drank from Memory instead of Lethe. The Sealers are hunting for the lake of Mnemosyne, and so is the thing under the First Door.
Why it's in
It gives Lethe an opposite. Real buried instructions for keeping your memory after death are the kind of detail fans dig into for years.
HistoryMythNewLivadeia (ancient Lebadea), central Greece · active from at least the 6th century BC; described c. AD 170

The oracle of Trophonius: the two real springs

The story
At Lebadea, beside the river Hercyna, people went down into a cave to consult the hero Trophonius. The travel writer Pausanias, who went down himself, describes the ritual: before descending, the visitor drank from a spring called Lethe, to forget everything they had been thinking, and then from a spring called Mnemosyne, to remember what they would see below. People came back up shaken, sometimes unable to laugh for days. The springs and the river gorge can still be visited in the town of Livadeia.
In LETHE
The place where the Sealers took their name for the other world. The order existed long before Greece, but its Greek chapter guarded the cave at Lebadea, and the oracle's ritual was their test: drink Lethe and forget, or drink Mnemosyne and see. Those who saw too much didn't come back up. The Greek word stuck, and the order has used it ever since. Tomas Vey explains this to Nysa in Scene 11, as if it were only history.
Why it's in
It's the one place where the names Lethe and Mnemosyne are attached to real, visitable water. It answers "why is a Pakistani family's story called by a Greek word" without changing the story: the name belongs to the Sealers, not to the place.
MythNewMesopotamia to Persia · c. 2000 BC onward

The Water of Life in the Land of Darkness

The story
Almost every old civilization near our story imagined a hidden water or plant that defeats death, and nearly always someone just misses it.

Mesopotamia: in the Epic of Gilgamesh, the hero dives for a plant at the bottom of the sea that restores youth. While he bathes, a snake steals it.

The Alexander legends: in the Greek Alexander Romance, Alexander's cook stumbles on a spring in a land of darkness, and a dried fish he rinses in it comes back to life. Alexander never drinks.

Islamic and Persian tradition: the Quran (Surah al-Kahf, 18:83–98) tells of Dhul-Qarnayn, a righteous ruler who travels to the far west and the far east and builds a barrier against Gog and Magog. The Quran itself doesn't mention a water of life. That story comes from later tradition and Persian poetry, such as Nizami's Iskandarnameh and Ferdowsi's Shahnameh: the king searches the Land of Darkness (Zulumat) for the spring of life (Ab-e-Hayat), and his guide al-Khidr finds it and drinks, while the king does not. Commentators also link it to the Quran's account of Musa and al-Khidr, where a fish comes alive at the meeting of the two seas (18:60–63).

Elsewhere: amrita, the nectar of immortality in Hindu tradition, and the European Fountain of Youth, attached to Ponce de León only after his death.
Debated
Whether Dhul-Qarnayn was a prophet. Many scholars, including in Shia tradition, describe him as a righteous servant of God rather than a prophet, and who he was historically is argued over.
In LETHE
Proposal: somewhere in the deepest dark of Lethe there's one spring that does the opposite of the river. The Greeks called it the lake of Memory. Older peoples called it the water of life. Every culture remembered it, under a different name. It's what the Sealers fear most and what the thing under the First Door wants. Nysa's Firstlight comes from it. No holy figure appears; only the spring, the darkness and the search.
Why it's in
It joins East and West: one spring that Greek, Mesopotamian, Persian and Islamic storytellers all reached for. Players from each tradition recognize their own story, and nobody has to be told.
MythAncient Egypt · c. 2500–30 BC

The ren, the name that is part of the soul

The belief
Egyptians saw the soul as several parts, and one of them was the ren, the name. As long as a name was spoken or written, the person lived on. Destroying someone's name was a way of destroying them even after death, which is why names were carved deep in stone and why enemies chiseled them out.
In LETHE
The third and fourth laws: what the living name, the Grey can't take, and the erased can't move on. It also explains why the Sealers chisel names rather than simply killing people.
Why it's in
It makes erasure feel like a fate worse than death, and it's true to how real people thought.
MythNewChina · folk religion

Meng Po and the soup of forgetting

The story
In Chinese tradition, souls about to be reborn meet Meng Po, an old woman on the Bridge of Helplessness, who serves a soup that erases all memory of past lives. It's the same idea as Lethe, imagined on the other side of the world.
In LETHE
Proposal: a character in the Shoal of Names, an old woman who offers the newly lost a bowl of soup, and peace. Nysa refuses it.
Why it's in
It shows the idea is human, not just Greek, and it gives players in China and East Asia, one of the world's largest gaming markets, something of their own in the world.
HistoryNewNorthern Portugal · c. 137 BC

The Limia, the real river Lethe

What happened
Roman soldiers marching under the general Decimus Junius Brutus refused to cross the river Limia, believing it was the Lethe and that they'd forget their homes. According to Roman writers, Brutus crossed alone, then called each soldier by name from the far bank to prove his memory was intact. They followed him.
In LETHE
Proposal: a key scene and a mechanic. Calling a person's name across the water brings them back. Nysa's first rescue of Eesa could echo it.
Why it's in
A real army feared our myth. It's the ideal first "Erased" video.
MythIreland and Scotland · Celtic Christian tradition

Thin places

The belief
A "thin place" is somewhere the boundary between this world and the next feels close: an island, a ruin, a hilltop. The phrase is associated with Celtic Christian spirituality and became widely used in the last two centuries.
In LETHE
The doorways between the worlds, recast as places where something was deliberately forgotten.
Why it's in
A gentle, recognizable idea that lets the story happen anywhere, including the player's own city.
MythRome · Ovid, AD 8

The cave of sleep, where dreams come from Lethe

The story
In Book 11 of Ovid's Metamorphoses, the god of sleep lives in a dark, silent cave in the far land of the Cimmerians, where no sunlight reaches. A stream of the river Lethe flows out from under its rock. Around the god lie the empty shapes of dreams, and his sons, including Morpheus, carry them out to sleepers.
In LETHE
The calling: the forgotten send dreams upward from the river of forgetting, hoping someone will find them. Eesa followed his dreams in 2011; Nysa follows the same dreams in 2026. Each chapter opens with a playable dream.
Why it's in
It's the ancient root of the whole calling idea. In the original myth, dreams really do come from Lethe.
HistoryGreece · c. 400 BC onward

The dream temples of Asclepius

What happened
At healing sanctuaries of the god Asclepius, the most famous at Epidaurus, the sick slept overnight in a special hall, hoping for a dream that would heal them or tell them what to do. The practice is called incubation. Inscriptions at Epidaurus record the cures people claimed.
In LETHE
The Sealers' modern front is a sleep clinic that treats "recurring dreams": a dream temple turned inside out, built to make dreams stop.
Why it's in
It gives the Sealers' clinic a 2,400-year-old ancestor, and it's a ready-made "Erased" video.
MythHistoryArabia · pre-Islamic star lore onward

Banat Na'sh and the seven stars

The story
The seven bright stars we call the Big Dipper were known in Arabic star lore as Banat Na'sh, "the daughters of the bier". In one version of the legend, they are mourners circling the sky forever, searching. Many of their names in Western astronomy still come from Arabic: Dubhe ("bear"), Merak ("loins"), Phecda ("thigh"), Megrez ("root of the tail"), Mizar ("girdle") and Alkaid, from "the leader of the daughters of the bier". In the real sky, Merak and Dubhe are the "pointer stars": a line through them points to the North Star.
In LETHE
Eesa's star map. He leaves one of the seven stars in each layer. Players find the pointers last, Merak in Chapter 6 and Dubhe in Chapter 7, and only then does the map point to the Spring in the Dark.
Why it's in
It's real astronomy kids can go outside and find, and a puzzle that runs across the whole saga.

Part two

The places

HistoryDebatedSoutheastern Turkey · c. 9500–8000 BC

Göbekli Tepe

What happened
A hilltop of massive T-shaped stone pillars carved with foxes, boars, snakes and vultures, built by hunter-gatherers before farming, pottery or writing. A 1963 survey noticed it and dismissed it as a medieval cemetery. In 1994 the archaeologist Klaus Schmidt recognized what it was, and excavation began in 1995. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018. Nearby sites such as Karahan Tepe show it wasn't alone.
Debated
Schmidt believed the enclosures were deliberately buried. More recent work suggests the fill may have come partly from slope slides and gradual processes. It's also a favorite of fringe "lost civilization" theories, which archaeologists push back on strongly.
In LETHE
The First Door, and the site of Nysa's dig. The Sealers' first act was to bury it.
Why it's in
It's among the oldest monumental sites known, it rewrote the history books, and its burial is a real open question. We present it as fiction inspired by a mystery, never as a claim about what really happened.
HistoryCentral Turkey · c. 1650–1180 BC

Hattusa and the forgotten Hittites

What happened
The Hittites ran one of the great empires of the Bronze Age from their capital, Hattusa. Their treaties call on "the thousand gods of Hatti" as witnesses. Their treaty with Egypt after the Battle of Kadesh is one of the oldest known peace treaties, and a replica hangs at the United Nations. Around 1180 BC the capital was abandoned and burned. Then the world forgot them: for nearly 3,000 years they were a name in the Bible and little else, until excavators found their royal archive of clay tablets in 1906 and the scholar Bedřich Hrozný deciphered their language in 1915.
In LETHE
Hattusa of the Thousand Gods, a region where a whole empire and its gods still stand.
Why it's in
A superpower that history forgot completely and then found again. It's the clearest real example of what Lethe is.
HistoryDebatedEastern Mediterranean · c. 1200–1150 BC

The Bronze Age Collapse and the Sea Peoples

What happened
Within a few decades, the Hittite empire, Mycenaean Greece, the trading city of Ugarit and others collapsed. At his temple at Medinet Habu, Pharaoh Ramesses III recorded his victory over invaders known today as the "Sea Peoples", in his eighth year, around 1177 BC, the date historian Eric Cline used as the title of his book. Afterwards Greece lost the art of writing for about four centuries.
Debated
The cause. Drought, earthquakes, broken trade networks, migration and war have all been proposed, probably in combination. Who the Sea Peoples were is still argued over.
In LETHE
The last open war between the worlds, and Act III's playable flashback. The Sea Peoples were the erased coming home.
Why it's in
A civilization so shaken it forgot how to write. That's the Great Forgetting with real evidence behind it.
HistoryDebatedEgypt · c. 285 BC – AD 391

The Library of Alexandria

What happened
Founded under the early Ptolemies, it aimed to collect every book in the world. It wasn't lost in one night. A fire during Julius Caesar's war in Alexandria in 48 BC destroyed many scrolls. Funding cuts, purges of scholars, fighting under Emperor Aurelian in the 270s, and the destruction of the Serapeum, its daughter library, in AD 391 ended it over centuries.
Debated
How much burned in 48 BC. A later story that the library was burned on a caliph's order after the Arab conquest first appears more than 500 years after the events, and most historians treat it as legend. We don't use it.
In LETHE
Alexandria Below, the library that lived, and its last librarian, Ione.
Why it's in
The most famous loss of knowledge in history. Everyone wishes it had survived, so in LETHE it did.
HistoryNewBaghdad · c. 800–1258

The House of Wisdom and the river of ink

What happened
Under the Abbasid caliphs, especially al-Ma'mun (813–833), Baghdad's Bayt al-Hikma drew scholars who translated Greek, Persian and Indian works and built on them. Al-Khwarizmi's work in Baghdad gave us the words "algebra" and "algorithm". In February 1258 the Mongol army under Hulagu sacked Baghdad. Chroniclers say so many books were thrown into the Tigris that the river ran black with ink.
In LETHE
Proposal: a second great library in Lethe, built on a river of ink, rival and sister to Alexandria Below.
Why it's in
A real river that carried knowledge away: Lethe made literal. It also brings the Islamic Golden Age into the world as history, not religion.

Part three

The erased people

HistoryEgypt · reigned c. 1353–1336 BC

Akhenaten, the heretic king

What happened
Born Amenhotep IV, he turned Egypt's religion toward a single sun-disk, the Aten, and built a new capital at Amarna. After his death, later rulers tore down his city, reused its stone blocks elsewhere, and left him off the official king lists. He was largely unknown until the 19th century. The Amarna letters, diplomatic tablets found by a local woman in 1887, helped bring his world back.
In LETHE
The Sun King Below, who leads the Return.
Why it's in
A visionary that his own country tried to unmake. A villain with a reason audiences can understand.
HistoryDebatedEgypt · reigned c. 1479–1458 BC

Hatshepsut, the queen who was chiseled

What happened
One of Egypt's most successful rulers: she built the great temple at Deir el-Bahari and sent a famous trading expedition to the land of Punt. About twenty years after her death, under Thutmose III, her statues were smashed and dumped in a quarry, and her images were cut from monuments. Archaeologists later pieced the statues back together. In 2007 her mummy was identified, partly through a single tooth.
Debated
Why she was erased. Many historians now think it was about the royal succession rather than personal hatred.
In LETHE
The political heart of the Chiseled Court. She wants her name back.
Why it's in
A powerful woman history tried to delete, and who was found again, down to a tooth.
HistoryRome · AD 189–211

Geta and the painting with a missing face

What happened
Geta ruled Rome jointly with his older brother Caracalla until December 211, when Caracalla had him killed, according to the historian Cassius Dio in their mother Julia Domna's arms. His name was cut from inscriptions and his portraits destroyed, and Dio reports thousands of his supporters were killed. One painted family portrait survives, the Severan Tondo, now in Berlin. It shows the imperial family, and one face has been scraped away.
In LETHE
The boy emperor of the Chiseled Court. The Severan Tondo is the ancient twin of Nysa's photograph.
Why it's in
A real family picture with a brother erased from it. It's the opening image of our story, made 1,800 years ago.
HistoryNewSoviet Union · 1937–1940

The commissar who vanished from the photo

What happened
Nikolai Yezhov ran Stalin's secret police during the Great Purge. After he fell from power and was executed in 1940, he was retouched out of official photographs, including one where he walks beside Stalin along a canal. In the edited version, there's only water where he stood.
In LETHE
Proof the Sealers' methods reach the modern age. A strong beat for the "Erased" series and the alternate-reality game.
Why it's in
It connects ancient erasure to the 20th century, and shows that removing a person from a picture really happens.

Part four

Burnings and vanishings

In the story, these are the Sealers' fingerprints across history.

HistoryDebatedChina · 213 BC

The burning of the books

What happened
Under the first emperor of China, Qin Shi Huang, histories and philosophy outside approved subjects were ordered burned. The traditional account adds that scholars were buried alive. Historians debate how much of that later story is true.
In LETHE
A Sealer operation in the secret history: the empire that unified China while making it forget.
Why it's in
It shows the war was global from the start, not just around the Mediterranean.
HistoryYucatán, Mexico · 1562

The Maya books burned at Maní

What happened
The Spanish friar Diego de Landa ordered Maya books and sacred objects burned at Maní in 1562. He later wrote one of the main early accounts of Maya culture. Only four Maya codices are known to survive today.
In LETHE
A future region: the lost libraries of the Maya, still standing in Lethe.
Why it's in
A whole written tradition reduced to four books. It brings Latin America into the world through a real, painful loss.
HistoryDebatedNorth Carolina · 1587–1590

Roanoke and the word CROATOAN

What happened
In 1587 about 115 English colonists settled on Roanoke Island. Their governor, John White, sailed to England for supplies, and war with Spain delayed his return until 1590. He found the settlement abandoned, "CROATOAN" carved into a post and "CRO" on a tree. Among the missing was his granddaughter Virginia Dare, the first English child born in the Americas.
Debated
Their fate. A leading theory is that they joined the Croatoan people on nearby Hatteras Island. Archaeologists are still digging.
In LETHE
The one Thin Place the Sealers failed to close in time.
Why it's in
America's most famous mystery, and a familiar hook for North American players. The Croatoan people are never treated as the threat.

Part five

The doors opening now

History2018 – today

Lasers under the jungle

What happened
LiDAR, laser scanning from aircraft, can see through forest to the ground. In 2018 a survey in northern Guatemala revealed more than 60,000 previously unknown Maya structures. In 2024 a doctoral student found a large lost Maya city, which his team named Valeriana, in survey data that had been gathered for other purposes and was freely available online.
In LETHE
Why the story happens now: technology is uncovering the doors faster than the Sealers can bury them.
Why it's in
It makes the present day the most dangerous moment in twelve thousand years, and it's true.

What we avoid

Lines we don't cross

  • No false history in public. Fringe theories about Göbekli Tepe and "lost civilizations" draw strong criticism from archaeologists. Our fiction is clearly fiction, and our facts are checked.
  • No discredited myths presented as fact. For example, the late legend of a caliph ordering Alexandria burned.
  • No real peoples as villains. Not the Croatoan, not the Mongols as a people, not any nation, sect or faith. The Sealers belong to no real group.
  • No holy figures as characters, and nothing sacred used as a game power.
  • Respect for living heritage. Hattusa and Göbekli Tepe are real heritage sites in Turkey. We use real places with care, and the "Erased" series should point people toward visiting and protecting them.

Founder's notes · private

The quiet threads

Not for public material. Never explained on screen.

  • The theme itself. Remembering the wronged as a duty, and a sister who bears witness for her brother before people in power. It sits at the heart of the story without naming where it comes from.
  • Şanlıurfa. Nysa's dig base is the city nearest Göbekli Tepe. Its old center has the sacred fish pools linked to the story of Ibrahim and the fire, honored by Muslims, Christians and Jews. It appears as a setting, with no comment.
  • The scholar who saved books. When Baghdad fell in 1258, the scholar Nasir al-Din al-Tusi was in the conquerors' service and is said to have rescued a great many manuscripts for his new observatory and library at Maragheh. A complicated figure who served a conqueror to save knowledge, and a strong inspiration for Ione's backstory.
  • Nysa's details. The silver ring with the red stone. A quiet word before hard moments. A grandmother's voice. Never commented on.
  • The spring in the dark. The water of life in the Land of Darkness, from the tradition around Dhul-Qarnayn and al-Khidr, is the source of Nysa's Firstlight. It's shown only as a spring, a darkness and a search. No holy figure appears or is named in the game.
  • The seven stars. The daughters of the bier circle the sky forever, searching. A sister searching for her brother, one star at a time. Never explained.
  • The music. A lament-style melody in the main theme.

Now in the bible (v0.4)

  • The Spring in the Dark as the bottom of Layer 7, the source of Nysa's Firstlight, and Eesa's last gift to her.
  • The calling: the forgotten send dreams upward. Eesa answered in 2011; the dreams reach Nysa in 2026. The first dream came from the bottom.
  • The time rule: time moves only for the remembered. Eesa grew up because his sister never forgot him.
  • Two timelines, NOW and THEN, fifteen years apart, in the same places.
