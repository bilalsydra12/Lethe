### SCENE 1 · NOW · "The Painted-Over Child"

> **Friday 5 June 2026 · 6:40 pm**
> The Ellery Museum, Upper East Side, New York · Paintings Conservation Studio, 5th floor
> 26 °C outside, humid and still. Thunderstorms forecast after ten.

The studio was the only room in the Ellery that faced north, and at this hour it was the best room in the city.

The sun had swung far enough west that it no longer came through the windows at all. It came *off* things: off the brick of the building across 79th Street, off the copper flashing on its roof, off a water tower that had been painted silver some time in the last century and never again. What reached the studio was light that had already been used once, soft and even, without shadows. Conservators had been asking for north light for four hundred years. Nysa Ali had been asking for it since she was nineteen, and now she had it every evening, and on most evenings she forgot to notice.

Tonight she noticed. Tonight everything had an edge.

The room smelled of three things, always in the same order. First the solvent, sharp and clean, like a cut apple left too long. Then the coffee, burnt, from the machine nobody descaled. Last, underneath both, the smell of old paintings themselves: linseed and dust and something like the inside of a church.

There were four easels in the studio and only one painting that mattered. *The Hartley Family*, oil on canvas, 1871, 152 by 198 centimetres, lay flat on the big padded table under the magnifying lamp, because a painting that size and that old could not be trusted upright any more. It had come to the museum in 1952 as a gift from a Hartley great-granddaughter, and had hung on the third floor for seventy years under a varnish that had slowly gone the colour of strong tea.

In the painting, Edmund Hartley, a shipping merchant, stood in a black frock coat with one hand inside his waistcoat, the way men stood when they wanted to be remembered as men who had built things. His wife Clara sat beside him in grey silk. Four children were arranged around them in descending height like organ pipes: two boys in sailor collars, a girl with a hoop, a baby on the mother's lap. Behind them hung a heavy green curtain, and to the far left of the group stood an empty chair.

Nysa had always hated that chair.

She had never said so out loud. It was not a professional opinion. But she had first seen *The Hartley Family* on a school trip when she was fourteen, newly arrived in New York, and the chair had bothered her then and it bothered her now. It was a good chair, mahogany, with a carved back. Clara Hartley's right hand rested on top of it. Not on her husband's arm, not on her baby: on the back of an empty chair, gently, the way you would rest your hand on a child's shoulder to keep them still for a painter.

*Why would you paint that,* she had thought at fourteen. *Why would anyone want to remember an empty chair.*

---

She wore what she always wore on a cleaning day: a grey cotton work apron, stiff and blotched with a decade of other people's mistakes, over a lavender T-shirt that said nothing at all. Her hair, dark brown that turned almost auburn under the lamp, was twisted up and held in place with a yellow pencil, but it was a losing battle; the curls at the ends kept falling out of the twist and down against her neck, and she kept pushing them back with her wrist, because her hands were in white cotton gloves and the gloves were not allowed to touch her face. On her head she wore a binocular magnifying visor that made her look, Dana said, like a very elegant welder.

Under the cuffs of the gloves, if you looked, the nail polish showed. Pale violet with silver glitter in it, chipped at the corners. She had painted it on Sunday night watching something she couldn't remember, and she would not have admitted to anyone in the building that she had chosen it partly because it caught the light when she worked, like small stars moving over the surface of a painting.

Her scarf, lavender too, very thin, hung over the back of her chair. She had worn it in that morning in twenty-six degrees of humidity because she always wore it. Her mother had sent it.

---

She was cleaning the curtain.

Two weeks earlier she had taken the yellow varnish off the faces and hands, and the Hartleys had come up out of the tea-coloured dark like people surfacing from a lake. Clara's skin had turned out to be pink and freckled, not brown. The baby had blue eyes. The girl's hoop was red. Every painting she cleaned did this, and it never stopped being a small miracle: the past had been the right colour all along, and only the varnish had been old.

The curtain was different. Under ultraviolet light, the varnish over most of the painting glowed a milky green, the way old natural resin does. But in one area, a broad patch of curtain beside the empty chair, roughly the size and shape of a person sitting down, the UV showed a dark purple shadow. Something on top of the original paint. Retouching, almost certainly. A later restorer covering damage, a tear perhaps, or a flake loss. It happened all the time.

That was what the condition report said. Dana had written it in 2019. *Area of later overpaint in drapery, lower left. Likely historic repair. Remove if stable.*

Nysa dipped the swab.

It was a long bamboo skewer with a tight twist of cotton wool at one end, hand-rolled, the size of the tip of her little finger. She had rolled two hundred of them that morning and they stood in a jar like a bouquet. The solvent she had mixed herself after three days of tests on the edge of the canvas: mostly a gentle alcohol, with just enough of something stronger to bite. She touched the swab to the curtain and rolled it, never rubbed, rolled it slowly toward herself across a patch no bigger than a coin, lifted it, looked at it.

The cotton had turned the colour of moss.

She turned the swab to a clean side, rolled again, looked again. She dropped it in the waste tin and took another. Roll. Look. Drop. This was the whole art of it and the whole discipline: you never removed more than the swab told you to. A painting could not be put back. Whatever you took away was gone forever, so you went slowly, so slowly that people who watched you work got bored and left, and you kept going after they'd gone.

Over three hours the green curtain had gone from a flat, heavy, slightly too-bright green to something else underneath. Not curtain. Not damage either. The paint underneath was a different green, warmer, finer, and then not green at all.

There was a seam of white.

She stopped. She lifted the visor, rubbed her eyes with the back of her wrist, put the visor back down.

It was a collar. A small boy's collar, white, starched. Above it, the soft edge of a jaw.

▸ **PLAY · The Cleaning**

The player controls a single swab over the canvas, in close-up.

- **Right trigger** sets the pressure. **Left stick** rolls the swab. Rolling *toward* you lifts overpaint; scrubbing back and forth damages it.
- The swab tip shows what it's taking: green means overpaint (good), **any other colour** means you are lifting original paint, and the controller pulses a warning. Three warnings and Dana leans in: *"Easy. It's been here a hundred and fifty years. It can wait another minute."*
- A **UV torch** (face button) shows the dark patch of overpaint, so the player can see the edges of the work area.
- There is **no timer and no score.** The mini-game ends when the overpaint is gone. Players who rush still finish, but the face underneath comes out with a small scar on the cheek that stays on the painting for the rest of the game, and Dana mentions it in Scene 10.
- **What it teaches:** patience, precision, and that what you remove is gone. The chisel later in the game is the exact opposite: fast, violent and paid for with yourself.

By a quarter to seven the face was clear.

A boy, about eleven or twelve, seated in the mahogany chair with his body turned slightly toward his mother. Dark hair, longer than the other boys', falling over his forehead. A thin, serious face. He was not looking at the painter, as every other Hartley was. He was looking up and out of the frame, to the top left, toward something only he could see. And on his shoulder, very lightly, where Nysa had always seen it rest on the empty chair, lay his mother's hand.

Clara Hartley had not been touching a chair. She had been holding on to a son.

Nysa sat back. The stool scraped. In the quiet studio it sounded like something tearing.

Somebody had painted him out. Not a hundred years later, not a careless restorer. The overpaint was old; it had cracked along with the rest of the picture. Somebody had painted the curtain right over a living child while the paint underneath was still young enough to take it, and had left the mother's hand exactly where it was, resting on nothing, so that for a hundred and fifty years the Hartley family had stood in their parlour beside an empty chair, and nobody had ever asked why.

Her right hand was shaking. She watched it shake with a kind of detached interest, as if it belonged to someone else. She put the swab down in the tray so she wouldn't drop it onto the canvas.

"*Oh*," said Dana, behind her.

Dana Okafor was fifty-four, and had been the Ellery's senior paintings conservator for nineteen years. She was a broad, calm woman with short grey hair and reading glasses on a beaded chain that her granddaughter had made, red and yellow and blue, completely at odds with the rest of her. She had come in from the office next door with a mug in each hand, and she stopped dead a metre from the table and set both mugs down on a filing cabinet without looking where.

"Oh, *look* at him," Dana said softly. "Look at that. Nysa. Do you know what you've got here?"

Nysa said nothing.

"A lost child. They painted out a child." Dana put her glasses on and bent close, not touching. "It'll be a death. Has to be. He died after the sitting, and Edmund couldn't bear to look at him every day, so he had the painter cover him. It happened. It happened more than you'd think. My God. And the hand. They left her hand." She straightened up, beaming. "This is going in the show. I don't care if the catalogue's printed. We'll do an insert. Two weeks before opening, and you find *this*. You're shaking. Look at you. I'd be shaking."

"Yes," Nysa said.

"Go home. Seriously. Photograph it, cover it, go home. We'll do the X-ray Monday." Dana picked one mug up again and gave it to her, and Nysa held it in both hands without drinking. "Best thing that's happened in this room in years."

It wasn't excitement. Nysa knew what excitement felt like. This was a feeling she had had once before in her life, when she was ten years old, and had spent fifteen years making sure she would never have again: the feeling of seeing a space in the world exactly the shape of a person, and being the only one in the room who knew a person had ever been there.

The boy in the painting went on looking up and away, toward the top left corner, where there was nothing at all.

---

On the corkboard beside the table, pinned in a neat grid, were the printouts for the exhibition panels. The exhibition opened in fifteen days. *ERASED: Faces History Removed*, said the banner design in heavy black capitals, with a long thin gap between the two words, as if a letter had been scraped away.

Nysa had written half the panels herself.

Top left: **the Severan Tondo**, a round painting on wood from about the year 200, now in Berlin. The Roman emperor Septimius Severus, his wife Julia Domna, and their two young sons. One son, Caracalla, still looked out at you. The other, Geta, had been murdered by his brother in the year 211, and afterwards his face had been scraped off the painting, smeared over, rubbed down to the bare wood. Romans had a phrase for it, a legal punishment: *damnatio memoriae*. The condemnation of memory. Geta's name had been chiselled off monuments across the empire. It had been a crime to say it aloud.

Next to it: **the photograph from 1937**, Stalin walking along the bank of the Moscow–Volga canal with three men. In the second print, from a few years later, there were only three of them. Nikolai Yezhov, the head of the secret police, had been arrested, shot, and then removed from the photograph so carefully that the water behind him had been painted back in.

She had spent four months reading about the people history had deleted. Nobody had found it strange that she wanted to.

---

Her phone buzzed against the steel of the trolley.

It was propped on its side against a jar of swabs, face up. The screen said **Ammi** with a little green video icon, and above it, the photograph Nysa had set for her mother's contact years ago: Syd Ali at a cousin's wedding, laughing, a plate of something in her hand, her pink dupatta slipping off her hair.

Nysa looked at the time. Ten to seven in New York meant ten to four in the morning in Chandsar. Her mother would have just finished her dawn prayer.

She pulled off one glove with her teeth and took the call.

The screen filled with blue. The kitchen in Chandsar before sunrise, lit only by the tube light over the stove, which flickered, as it had flickered for twenty years. Behind her mother's head the window was a square of dark navy going grey at the bottom, and Nysa could see the black shapes of the deodar trees against it, and the white edge of a mountain. Her mother's face came very close to the camera, then too far away, then close again, as it always did. She still had her white prayer dupatta wrapped around her hair and pinned under the chin, and her face had that particular stillness it had for a few minutes after praying, before the day got hold of her.

"*Haan,* there you are. Can you see me? Nysa? *Beta,* can you see me?"

"I can see you, Ammi."

"You look tired. You're not eating. What is that on your head? Are you at work? It's Friday night, why are you at work, it's Friday night, you should be with people."

Nysa pushed the magnifying visor up onto her hair. "I'm finishing something. Why are you calling at four in the morning?"

"Because I'm awake at four in the morning. When else would I call? Your father is asleep. Listen." Her mother took a breath, and Nysa watched her face change, watched her decide to be cheerful. "We sold the house."

The kettle behind her mother began to tick as it heated.

"You *what?*"

"I told you we were thinking. I told you at Eid."

"You said you were thinking about *thinking*."

"Well, we thought. Your father got the post at the university, Nysa. In Islamabad. *Professor* Ali." She said the word as if she were tasting it. "And the winters, *Allah*, last winter the road was closed for nineteen days. Nineteen days. If something happened to one of us, what? A helicopter? Your Abbu found a flat in F-11, with a balcony and a lift. A *lift*." She said it the way someone else might say *a swimming pool*. "A man from Lahore is buying the house. He's going to make it into a guest house for tourists. He takes the keys on the twentieth."

"The twentieth of *June*?"

"So we need you to come."

Nysa looked across the studio at the boy in the painting. From this angle, under the lamp, his eyes seemed to follow the line of the window frame.

"Ammi, I can't just..."

"Your things are here. Your school things, your drawings, all those boxes. I'm not throwing away your drawings, I'm not a monster. And somebody has to clear out the store room. Your father can't lift. I can't lift. And I don't know what half the things in there even *are*."

Nysa was quiet for a moment.

"Which room?"

"The store room."

"Which store room, Ammi?"

Her mother laughed, a little impatiently, and the camera jolted as she turned to the kettle. "*Kaunsa?* What do you mean which? The store room. At the back, next to your room, with the window over the lake. The junk room. It has always been the store room."

The window over the lake.

Nysa knew that room. She knew the view from its window better than the view from her own: the slope of the garden going down to the water, the flat stones at the edge where you could stand without getting your feet wet, the reflection of Malika Parbat on calm mornings so perfect you could have turned the world upside down and not noticed. She knew the room had a crack in the plaster above the door shaped like the River Indus on a map. She knew the room had smelled of pencil shavings and wet wool socks and the cheap cherry chewing gum from the Naran bazaar. She knew that for fourteen years of somebody's life, that room had not been a store room at all.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. I'll come."

"Really? You'll come?" Her mother's whole face opened, and Nysa felt it in her chest like a hand. "*Shukr hai.* Thank God. I'll tell your father. He'll drive down to get you. Book it, okay? Book it tonight, don't wait, the prices go up. And eat something. You're so thin I can see the kitchen through you."

"I'll eat something, Ammi."

"*Allah hafiz, jaan.*" God keep you, my life.

"*Allah hafiz.*"

The screen went dark. In the black glass, for a moment, Nysa could see her own face with the lamp behind it, and over her shoulder, very small, the painting on the table.

▸ **PLAY · The Call**

A short, fully voiced cutscene with **two light choices** that don't change the plot, only how Nysa sounds:

1. When Ammi says *"We sold the house"*: **[Angry]** "You *what?*" / **[Quiet]** "...Oh." Both continue the same way.
2. When Ammi says *"the store room"*: the camera holds on Nysa's face. **[Ask]** "Which room?" (default; plays as written) / **[Say nothing]** Nysa stays silent; Ammi fills the gap by describing the room herself ("the junk room, with the window over the lake"), and the scene ends the same way.

**What it plants:** the store room; the window over the lake; the date, 20 June (the same day *Erased* opens); and the first sign that the people Nysa loves cannot see what she can.

She photographed the painting at full resolution under raking light, then under UV, then in normal light again, the way she had been trained. She laid the protective sheet over it and smoothed it with the flat of her hand, careful not to press. She washed her hands at the deep steel sink in the corner and dried them on a paper towel and stood looking at nothing.

Dana had gone. The studio was dark except for the lamp over the table and the red standby light on the coffee machine. Outside, the copper roof across the street had gone dull brown. The sky over Central Park, four blocks west, had turned a heavy yellow-grey, the colour of a bruise healing, and very far off, somewhere over New Jersey, the first thunder moved like furniture in an upstairs room.

She put her scarf around her neck, although it was far too hot for it.

At the door she stopped and looked back, and for a second, in the half dark, she could have sworn that the shape under the white sheet had more than five figures in it, and that one of them was looking up.

### SCENE 2 · NOW · "Four Faces"

> **Friday 5 June 2026 · 11:20 pm**
> Nysa's apartment · 4th floor, a pre-war walk-up on 37th Avenue, Jackson Heights, Queens
> Thunderstorm. 23 °C and falling. Rain hard enough to hear over the air conditioner.

The storm reached Queens at a quarter past ten and sat down over it.

By eleven the rain on 37th Avenue was coming down in sheets, bouncing knee-high off the roofs of parked cars, turning the gutters into fast brown streams that carried paper cups and a single yellow flip-flop toward the drain on the corner of 75th Street. Two blocks south the 7 train went by on its elevated track over Roosevelt Avenue, and every time it passed, lit up and nearly empty, the rain in front of it turned briefly gold.

The building had no lift. Nysa had carried every one of her possessions up its four flights of marble stairs, worn into soft dips in the middle by a hundred years of feet, and she knew each landing by its smell: the first floor of fried onions and cumin, the second of a little girl's strawberry shampoo, the third of nothing at all, the fourth of her own front door, which smelled faintly of the lavender oil she put on the doormat so it wouldn't smell of the stairs.

Inside, the apartment was one long room and a short one, and it had been chosen entirely for its windows. Two of them looked out over the avenue. One opened onto the fire escape, where nine pots of plants stood in a row on the iron slats: basil, mint, a lemon tree that had never produced a lemon, three kinds of fern and a pink geranium that had survived two winters out of spite.

Purple string lights ran along the top of the bookshelves. They were the first thing she had bought for the apartment, before a bed, and she had lived for a month with a mattress on the floor and the ceiling glowing violet, and been happier than she could explain.

Everywhere else there were frames.

Old frames, mostly empty, leaning in stacks against every wall. Gilded frames with chipped corners, carved oak frames, a small round silver frame from Bombay that had held somebody's grandmother. People threw frames out, and Nysa could not walk past a frame on the street without picking it up, cleaning it, regluing the joints, filling the losses, toning them back in with watercolour. She did it at night, at the table by the window, while podcasts about other things played in her ear. She had restored forty-one frames and sold none of them. They leaned against the walls of the apartment like a gallery waiting for its paintings.

---

She had changed into a pair of old grey sweatpants and an oversized purple hoodie that said NYU CONSERVATION CENTER in faded white letters, the sleeves pulled down over her hands. Her hair was down now, still damp from the shower, the curls at the ends gone tight and springy the way they did in the rain. She had taken off her make-up. Without it her face looked younger, and paler, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with tonight.

She had eaten, because she had promised: two slices of cold pizza standing at the counter, and half a mango.

Now she sat cross-legged on the rug in the middle of the room with her back against the sofa, a cup of chai going cold on the floor beside her, and her laptop open on a flight search she wasn't reading. On the screen, the cheapest route to Islamabad left JFK on Thursday evening, changed in Doha, and landed at 3:05 on Saturday morning. Somewhere in the back of her mind she was already counting: Thursday the eleventh. Saturday the thirteenth. The twentieth, the keys, the opening.

She didn't book it. Not yet.

Instead she reached up to the sofa behind her and pulled down her sketchbook.

On the top shelf of the bookcase, between a row of conservation textbooks and a glass jar of sea glass, stood a rocket.

It was made of Lego, about thirty centimetres tall, and it was not built from any set. You could tell. The bricks were from everywhere: white and grey mostly, but with red and blue and one bright green piece where a white one had obviously run out. The fins were made of four sloped roof bricks from a house. The nose cone was a little yellow traffic cone from a city set. Someone had taken the trouble to give it three stages that came apart, the way real rockets did, and the top stage had a tiny window with a Lego figure inside it, a spaceman in a white helmet with the visor scratched away by years of small fingers.

It had come with her from Pakistan at fourteen in her hand luggage, wrapped in a jumper, and she had never once been able to say why.

Nobody she had ever lived with had asked about it. That was the strange thing. Not her roommate at NYU, who had asked about everything. Not the man she'd dated for a year at twenty-two, who had once spent forty minutes asking about her nail polish. People looked at the bookshelf, and read the spines of the books, and picked up the sea glass and held it to the light, and their eyes went over the rocket the way water goes around a stone. It wasn't that they ignored it. They simply didn't see it, and if she pointed it out, they said *oh, cute*, and a minute later it was as if she had never said anything.

She had a theory about the rocket. She had a theory about the cricket bat in the store room at home, and about the second toothbrush mug in the upstairs bathroom in Chandsar, the blue one, which her mother had kept for fifteen years and never once wondered about. She had never said the theory aloud, because it was the kind of theory that got you a referral.

*Things stay,* the theory went. *When a person is taken out of the world, the records go. The photographs in the album, the name in the school register, the memories in other people's heads: all of that goes. But the things they made, the things they touched, don't know how to disappear. They just stay where they were left, and nobody can explain them, and after a while nobody tries.*

The sketchbook was black, A4, hardbound, the eleventh of its kind. She kept the photograph in the back, between the last page and the cover, where it stayed flat.

She slid it out.

It was an ordinary print from an ordinary shop in Mansehra, glossy, the colours already turning faintly orange at the edges, with a crease across one corner where it had once been folded and then very carefully unfolded. On the back, in her father's handwriting, in blue biro: *Saiful Muluk, Aug. 2010.*

In the photograph the lake was so still that the mountain stood in it twice. Malika Parbat, the Queen of Mountains, white and enormous, and below it her reflection, white and enormous and upside down. In front of the water, on the stony shore, a family stood in a row in their jackets. It had been cold even in August.

Her father, Alex, at thirty-nine: tall, his long black hair combed straight back and tied at the nape, a full dark beard, a brown leather jacket, grinning so widely his eyes had creased almost shut. On his shoulders, holding onto his ears with both hands, a nine-year-old girl in a bright pink anorak with the hood up, laughing so hard her eyes had disappeared. Beside them her mother, Syd, at thirty-six, her long caramel-brown hair loose and blowing across her face, a green shawl around her shoulders, small gold jhumkas catching the sun, one hand raised to shield her eyes and the other half-lifted toward the girl, as if she were about to say *careful*, which she probably was.

And at the end of the row, standing a little apart from the others, a boy.

He was thirteen in the photograph, and tall for it, all elbows and wrists. His black hair needed cutting and the wind had lifted it on one side. He wore a navy fleece zipped up to his chin and held a blue plastic telescope against his chest with both arms, the way younger children hold a toy they're afraid someone will take. He wasn't looking at the camera. He was looking up and to the left, at the sky over the mountain, at something only he could see.

Four faces.

Nysa had stopped showing people the photograph when she was thirteen. The last person she had shown it to was a school counsellor in Islamabad, a gentle woman with a large tissue box and a poster of a rainbow on her wall, who had looked at the photograph for a long, kind time and then said: *It's a lovely picture of you and your parents.* Nysa had said: *And my brother.* And the counsellor had looked at the photograph again, right at the end of the row, right at him, for several seconds, and then looked back up with her head on one side and an expression Nysa had seen many times since, on many faces, and had learned to dread. Pity, and patience, and a little alarm.

Afterwards the counsellor had written a word on her notepad. Nysa had read it upside down. *Imaginary?* With a question mark. And under it, underlined, *grief?*, which was the strangest part, because grief was for people who had died, and as far as the whole world was concerned nobody had died at all.

---

She looked at him for a long time.

Then she turned the sketchbook over and opened it from the front, and turned the pages slowly, one by one, the way she had on this night, or near it, every year for fifteen years.

Faces. Page after page of faces, in pencil, in charcoal, a few in watercolour. Strangers on the 7 train. Old men playing chess in Travers Park. The guard at the Ellery who sang under his breath. Dana, laughing. Her mother, from a video call, three times. And, every few pages, the same face, getting older.

She had started at eleven, the first June after, with a child's pencil and a child's hand: a big round head, the telescope bigger than his arm. She had drawn him the way he looked in the photograph, from memory and from the print, as carefully as she could, so that she wouldn't lose it. The next year she had drawn him again, and without quite deciding to, she had made him a year older: a little longer in the jaw, a little heavier in the brow. Then again. And again. Eleven sketchbooks, fourteen drawings, and you could watch her learn to draw across them as surely as you could watch him grow. A boy of fourteen became a boy of sixteen with a shadow on his upper lip. Then a young man of twenty, with his father's narrow nose and their mother's eyes. The latest, from last June, was a man of twenty-eight she had never met: black hair cut short, a short beard, the same serious mouth, the same way of looking slightly up and away, as if the person in front of him were less interesting than the sky.

On every drawing, in the bottom corner, in small careful letters, she had written the date and his age. *17.06.2012 · 15. 17.06.2013 · 16.* All the way to *17.06.2025 · 28.*

She had never once written his name. She was afraid that if she wrote it, she would look down one day and find it gone.

---

The thunder came very close then, directly overhead, a crack so loud the string lights seemed to flicker with it. Somewhere down on the avenue a car alarm started up, then stopped.

Nysa turned back to the photograph and laid it on the open page beside last year's drawing: the boy at thirteen, the man at twenty-eight, side by side.

In fifteen days the museum would open an exhibition about faces that history had scraped away, and hundreds of people would walk past Geta's ruined face and Yezhov's empty stretch of riverbank, and feel a small cold thrill of injustice, and go and have lunch. Tonight a boy had come up out of a green curtain under her own hands, a boy whose own mother had been made to rest her hand on an empty chair for a hundred and fifty years. And in fifteen days a man from Lahore would take the keys to a house in the mountains and turn a room with a crack shaped like the Indus into a guest bedroom for tourists, and whatever was still in that room would go into a skip.

The things stay, she thought. *Until someone throws them away.*

She pulled the laptop onto her knees and booked the flight.

Thursday the eleventh, 9:50 pm, JFK. Doha. Islamabad, Saturday, 3:05 am. One passenger. One checked bag. Seat 41K, a window.

Then she opened her email and wrote to Dana: *I need to take next week off. Family. The house in Pakistan has been sold and I have to clear my brother's*

She stopped. She looked at the cursor blinking after the word *brother's*.

She deleted it, letter by letter, and wrote: *the old store room.* And sent it.
